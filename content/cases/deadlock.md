## Situation

A fraud product had a $25M target riding on a new feature for acquiring banks. They wanted finer control over fraud thresholds, like holding newly acquired merchants to stricter rules for a while. Product and engineering had been debating the MVP for three months, mostly without talking to customers. A week before product’s deadline, engineering presented a completely different solution as if it was already decided. After that the two teams only spoke through their scrum leads. Morale was about what you’d expect.

## My role

I flagged it to leadership and proposed a two-day, in-person workshop with VPs from product, engineering, commercialization, and sales. On paper I was there to help frame things around the customer. The real goal was to lock scope and lower the temperature.

Beforehand I collected every team’s specs and merged them into one rough service blueprint. Everyone disliked it immediately, which was useful. It gave us permission to start over.

We rebuilt from the user journey. Every idea went on the wall, competing ones side by side, and old grievances went in a parking lot so they were heard without taking over the room. Then we plotted the conflicts on two maps: impact against certainty, and ease of use against feasibility. Most of the arguments turned into research questions and tech spikes.

## What changed

Scope was locked by the end of day one, with a beta to 10 customers who’d asked for it. The next morning the product and engineering leads, who hadn’t been on speaking terms, co-presented the plan to about 60 people. It shipped on track, and teams kept pulling up that wall whenever old ideas crept back in.

## What’s still hard

This only works if a sponsor will let you say the first draft is wrong on purpose.
